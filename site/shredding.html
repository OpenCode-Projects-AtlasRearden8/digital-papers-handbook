What needs the shredder, what can just be recycled, and why "shred everything" is doing too much.

The one question that decides everything

Does this paper contain something a stranger could use to spend my money, open an account as me, or pretend to be me?

  • Yes → shred.
  • No → recycling is fine.
  • Unsure? Shred. The shredder is never the wrong call; the recycling bin sometimes is.

That's it. Wedding invitations, coupon mailers, and the electric bill summary page can go straight into the blue bin. The shredder isn't for paper — it's for identifiers: account numbers, social security numbers, signatures, dates of birth.

Shred list (things you should shred)

  • Anything with your full account number on it — statements, convenience checks, pre-approved credit offers, old cards that expired
  • Anything with your Social Security number (why does so much paper have this on it)
  • Anything with signatures on blank or reusable forms
  • Old insurance cards, prescription labels, anything with full name + date of birth combos
  • Likewise: expired passes, shipping labels with address + order number, tax papers past their retention window

Recycle list (fine straight to the bin)

  • Deeply unremarkable junk mail: envelopes (not the contents), catalogs, flyers
  • Bill payment stubs without full account numbers
  • Instruction manuals for appliances past their return window (Keep or Toss covers whether you even need them at all)
  • Old calendars, school forms without identifiers, telephone books, greeting card envelopes

Things that go through a third door: actually destroying, not just shredding

The old government-issued papers of a deceased relative, spare expired passports/certificates that were officially replaced through an agency, and documents a notary or agency has told you to surrender: check with the agency first. Shredding can be the right move, or there can be a formal return process. When in doubt, ask.

How to shred, cheaply

  • Already have scissors? That's a shredder. Cut through account numbers and signatures specifically — five seconds of attitude with scissors beats an hour of procrastinating on buying a device.
  • A $30 strip-cut shredder covers a household. Micro-cut is nicer for SSNs but honestly optional.
  • Shred-it events at many libraries/banks twice a year take whole boxes — best for the big archive-purge.
  • Burning works but is a whole production; not recommended for indoor people or high-wind areas.

Digital shredding, same rules

When a digital file reaches its retention date:

  • Delete the file — standard delete from your drive is plenty for personal use.
  • If you want actual deletion of sensitive documents ("I want the bytes gone"), your synced drive's "deleted files" retention (usually 30 days) or your operating system's trash is the place to empty next.
  • For true destruction of the file history rather than the paper mindset: the document might be in email attachments, chat history, or old scans — those are the ones to remember when you're being thorough.

The anti-hoarding footnote

If you shred it and needed it... you didn't, usually. The retention table exists precisely so that the shredder is never a gamble. Err on keeping: PDFs cost nothing.
